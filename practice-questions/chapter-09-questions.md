## Chapter 9: Assessing Readiness and Leading Change Management — Practice Questions

**Q1.** A pilot AI deployment in the finance department has strong data quality and infrastructure, but employees openly say 'this tool is going to replace half of us.' Adoption has stalled even though training completion is 100%. Which readiness dimension was most likely under-assessed before rollout?

A) Infrastructure and licensing readiness
B) Data readiness
C) Skills readiness
D) Culture readiness (trust and psychological safety around AI)

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: D**

High training completion with stalled behavior change points to a culture/trust gap, not a knowledge or skills gap — employees have the ability but not the desire (ADKAR) because of unaddressed fear.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Infrastructure/licensing issues would show up as access errors or outages, not verbal resistance.
- Data readiness problems manifest as poor model output quality, not fear-based statements.
- Skills readiness is explicitly ruled out — training completion is 100%, so the ability component is not the blocker.

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q2.** An organization wants to select the first pilot group for an AI rollout across 12 departments. Which selection criterion BEST predicts pilot success and a positive signal for scale-up?

A) The department with the lowest current headcount, to minimize support burden
B) The department with a visible, engaged leader and a well-defined, repeatable use case
C) The department that requested the tool most recently
D) The department with the largest budget for licensing

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: B**

Pilot selection should optimize for a credible internal success story: engaged local leadership drives adoption velocity, and a repeatable use case produces measurable, transferable results for the scale phase.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Low headcount reduces risk but doesn't validate whether the use case scales or whether leadership will champion it.
- Recency of request has no bearing on readiness or leadership engagement.
- Budget size doesn't correlate with cultural readiness or use-case clarity.

</details>

**Q3.** Six months into an enterprise AI rollout, usage metrics show department managers approved the tool but never mention it in team meetings, and no manager uses it visibly. Adoption plateaus at 15%. What is the most likely root cause?

A) Insufficient technical infrastructure
B) Poor initial pilot department selection
C) Lack of active, visible executive/manager sponsorship
D) Missing role-based training content

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: C**

Sponsorship approval alone (passive) is not the same as active sponsorship (visible modeling, reinforcement, communication). Prosci research consistently identifies active sponsorship as the top driver of adoption velocity; its absence is the most common cause of stalled rollouts after a successful pilot.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Infrastructure issues would produce access/error complaints, not silent non-mention by leadership.
- Pilot selection affects early results, not a plateau six months into enterprise-wide deployment.
- Training gaps affect ability, but the scenario describes a visibility/reinforcement problem at the leadership level, not a skills complaint.

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q4.** Which mechanism BEST supports peer-level reinforcement and localized troubleshooting during a department-to-enterprise AI rollout?

A) A centralized help desk ticketing system
B) A single annual all-hands training event
C) A champions network of trained volunteer power-users embedded in each team
D) Mandatory e-learning modules with a completion certificate

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: C**

A champions network places trusted peers inside each team who provide just-in-time help, model correct usage, and surface local resistance early — reinforcing adoption in ways top-down training and centralized support cannot.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- A help desk handles technical tickets but doesn't build peer trust or reinforce behavior change day-to-day.
- A single annual event addresses awareness once but provides no ongoing reinforcement.
- E-learning completion measures knowledge transfer, not ability or sustained behavior change.

</details>

**Q5.** Employees complete all required training but still don't use the new AI tool in daily workflows because they say they 'don't trust the outputs enough to rely on them.' In the ADKAR model, which stage is unmet?

A) Awareness
B) Knowledge
C) Ability
D) Reinforcement

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: C**

Ability is the capacity to apply the skill and knowledge in real work — including confidence in output quality. Distrust of outputs blocks the practical application of otherwise-completed training, which is an ability gap, not a knowledge gap.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Awareness (understanding why the change is happening) is not the issue — employees clearly know the tool exists and why it was introduced.
- Knowledge (knowing how to use the tool) is confirmed complete via training completion.
- Reinforcement sustains behavior after adoption has already started; here adoption hasn't started, so reinforcement isn't yet the blocker.

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q6.** Which sequence represents the correct order for a low-risk, evidence-based AI adoption rollout?

A) Pilot → single department → additional departments → enterprise-wide
B) Enterprise rollout → department rollout → pilot → retrospective
C) Department rollout → pilot → enterprise-wide → champions network
D) Enterprise-wide announcement → pilot metrics collection → department rollout

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: A**

Sequencing should minimize risk and maximize learning: a contained pilot validates the use case, a single department tests scaled processes, additional departments confirm repeatability, and enterprise-wide rollout applies proven playbooks.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Starting at enterprise scale eliminates the ability to learn from a contained failure before it's costly.
- Running pilot after department rollout reverses the risk-reduction logic of sequencing.
- Announcing enterprise-wide before piloting exposes the whole organization to unvalidated risk.

</details>

**Q7.** A rollout team plans communications as a single kickoff email at launch with no further updates until a survey six months later. What is the most likely consequence, based on change management best practice?

A) Awareness will decay and reinforcement will be absent, causing usage to regress over time
B) Adoption will be unaffected since the initial email covers awareness
C) Skills readiness will improve automatically as employees explore independently
D) Executive sponsorship becomes unnecessary once the kickoff email is sent

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: A**

Change management requires a recurring communication cadence (e.g., regular updates, success stories, FAQs) to sustain awareness and provide reinforcement; a single message produces an initial awareness spike that decays without follow-up, and usage regresses toward pre-change behavior.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Awareness is not static — without repetition, message retention and behavior fade, especially amid competing priorities.
- Skills don't improve from silence alone; ability requires guided practice and support, not passive exploration.
- Sponsorship visibility must continue throughout the rollout, not end at kickoff — this is the opposite of best practice.

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q8.** In the context of organizational change management, what is the primary distinction between 'awareness' and 'desire' in the ADKAR model?

A) Awareness is a technical skill; desire is a leadership responsibility
B) Awareness is understanding why the change is needed; desire is the personal motivation to support and participate in it
C) Awareness applies only to executives; desire applies only to individual contributors
D) Awareness and desire are interchangeable terms for the same readiness stage

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: B**

Awareness is cognitive (knowing the business reason for the change); desire is emotional/motivational (personal willingness to engage). An employee can be fully aware of why AI is being adopted yet still lack the desire to participate.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Neither awareness nor desire is defined as a 'technical skill' — that maps to knowledge/ability instead.
- Both stages apply to every individual affected by the change, not segmented by role level.
- They are distinct, sequential stages in ADKAR — conflating them is a common but incorrect simplification.

</details>
